Every populated Maryland ZIP code scored for hurricane and flooding, with strong wind and tornado as smaller inputs, from the FEMA National Risk Index (December 2025 release) at census-tract resolution, weighted to where each ZIP's residents live, and grouped into four tiers. These are Maryland's two largest property perils in FEMA's own building-loss estimates: flooding, from tidal and storm-surge water around the Chesapeake Bay and the Atlantic coast to river and flash flooding in the stream valleys and the western mountains, and hurricane wind, which is highest on the Eastern Shore and in Southern Maryland. The cut points are the ones we use for Virginia, so the two states read on one scale. Your campaign is Auto, so the tiers matter in two ways: comprehensive coverage on every Auto quote, and the home quote that turns an Auto policy into a bundle. Section 04 is the recommended treatment for those home quotes, section 05 covers Baltimore & Central Maryland, your largest lead market, with the Washington suburbs alongside, and section 06 matches the tiers against the 585 GOAL Auto leads delivered from July 1 to November 28, 2025, 268 of them in Maryland. No geo-targeting changes have been made. The full ZIP-by-ZIP list ships as a spreadsheet with this report.

03

Scoring system

Public federal data at census-tract resolution

What goes into the score
  • Why these twoFEMA's expected annual building loss for Maryland ranks inland flooding ($466 million a year), hurricane ($108 million a year), earthquake ($36 million a year), coastal flooding ($27 million a year). Flooding, inland and coastal together, is 71% of all modeled building loss in the state and hurricane is next at 16%; hail ranks seventh. Flood is excluded from a standard home policy and quoted separately, but it is the largest modeled loss and the question that comes up on the call, so it stays in the score at full weight.
  • HurricaneNRI hurricane risk: modeled hurricane wind frequency and strength times the value exposed, scored 0 to 100 nationally. Florida and the Gulf Coast set the top of the national scale, and half of Maryland housing sits in ZIPs scoring 68.8 or less, so the cut points are set for the Chesapeake region.
  • FloodingNRI inland and coastal flooding risk: each ZIP takes the higher of its two scores, so a tidal ZIP on the Bay and a river ZIP in the mountains are measured on the same 0 to 100 scale. Half of Maryland housing sits in ZIPs scoring 29.0 or less.
  • Supporting inputsNRI strong wind and tornado risk: added to the composite score at lower weight, so ZIPs where several perils add up are caught. Hail is in the spreadsheet only.
  • AvailabilityCarrier models (roof age and material, construction, elevation, flood zone, distance to the water) are property-level and not published by ZIP. This composite uses public federal data on the same hazards.

Tier rules
  • ExtremeHurricane score 84+ or flooding score 88+
  • HighHurricane score 79+, flooding score 80+, or composite score 70+
  • ModerateHurricane score 71+, flooding score 62+, or composite score 58+
  • LowEverything else.
  • How Extreme is setThe cut points are the ones we use for Virginia, so a Maryland tier and a Virginia tier mean the same thing. In Maryland they mark about one home in ten as Extreme (9.9% of housing). The hurricane arm (84+) covers 8.0% of Maryland housing and the flooding arm (88+) covers 6.8%. High and Moderate widen each arm, and the composite catches ZIPs where several perils add up.

Home quotes: recommended treatment

Extreme tier gets an appetite check · High tier gets a wind, flood and deductible review

Check appetite · Extreme · 149 ZIPs · 9.9% of MD housing Wind, flood and deductible review · High · 49 ZIPs · 5.7% No change · 272 ZIPs · 84.4%
Extreme ZIPs hold 9.9% of Maryland housing, and 15.6% with the High tier. Your campaign is Auto, so these tiers do not limit who you reach; they shape the home quote that follows. Where new home business is not being written in a ZIP, the Auto policy still stands on its own; everywhere else, treat the Extreme and High tiers as a conversation: roof age and how wind is written, how the hurricane or wind deductible works, and whether the home needs or would benefit from a flood policy.
Home treatment by tier
  • ExtremeCheck appetite. Before offering the home quote, confirm new home business is being written in the ZIP. In waterfront and Eastern Shore ZIPs, ask the roof's age, whether the home has storm shutters or impact-rated openings, its elevation and flood zone, and explain how the hurricane deductible works; offer a flood quote. In river-flood ZIPs, ask how close the home sits to the river or creek and whether the lender requires flood, and offer a flood quote.
  • HighWind, flood and deductible review. Ask the year the roof was replaced, walk through the wind or hurricane deductible, often a percentage of the dwelling amount, and ask whether the home has ever taken on water. Offer flood as a follow-up quote.
  • ModerateNo change. Standard quote; a flood question is still worth asking anywhere near the water.
  • LowNo change. Standard quote.

07

Hurricane and flood in the conversation

Questions that make a Maryland Auto quote, and the home quote that follows, faster and stickier

Why it matters here
  • Wind99% of housing on the Eastern Shore and in Southern Maryland sits in ZIPs scoring 71+ for hurricane. Roof age and covering, storm shutters and how wind is written today belong in the first conversation, not the renewal.
  • DeductiblesNear the water, hurricane or wind deductibles are often a percentage of the dwelling amount. Explaining the dollar figure on the first call avoids a surprise after the first storm and builds trust.
  • Tidal flooding7% of Baltimore & Central Maryland housing sits in ZIPs scoring 80+ for coastal flooding, on the Bay shore and the tidal rivers. Flood is excluded from a standard home policy, and low-lying waterfront streets can flood at high tide or in a nor'easter without a hurricane at all, so a flood quote is a natural follow-up on every home quote near the water, inside or outside a mapped flood zone.
  • River flooding1.4% of Marylanders live in tracts FEMA rates high for inland flooding and 2.4% for coastal flooding. In Western Maryland and along the stream valleys of the Piedmont, ask how close the home is to the river or creek; flash flooding can reach homes well outside a mapped flood zone.
  • AutoFlood and storm damage to a car is paid under comprehensive coverage. On every Auto quote in a High or Extreme ZIP, confirm comprehensive is included; it is also the natural opening to quote the home.

Data & files

  • Risk sourceFEMA National Risk Index, census tract table, December 2025 release (hurricane, inland flooding, coastal flooding, strong wind, tornado, hail), all 1,463 Maryland tracts. The December 2025 release reports river flooding as inland flooding. Peril choice from the same release's expected annual building loss, summed across Maryland tracts.
  • GeographyU.S. Census Bureau 2020 ZIP Code Tabulation Areas and the 2020 ZCTA-to-tract relationship file. The file reports land-area overlaps, not population, so each tract's values are weighted into a ZIP by the tract's population times the share of its land inside the ZIP. Tract parts outside Maryland are not scored. Baltimore City is treated as its own county-equivalent. Housing counts from ACS 2020-2024.
  • LeadsAll 585 GOAL leads for this account, from its one Auto campaign, Jul 1 to Nov 28, 2025, counted by ZIP: 268 in Maryland, 287 in Virginia and 30 in Delaware. 265 of the 268 Maryland leads matched a populated Maryland ZIP. Virginia and Delaware ZIP data could not be downloaded for this report, so those leads are shown as counts; the Virginia tier mix uses our earlier Virginia ZIP tiers, which share the same cut points. No geo-targeting changes have been made.
  • LimitsA ZIP score describes the ZIP, not a house. Roof age, construction, elevation, flood zone and distance to the water decide each quote; the tiers decide where to ask first.
  • FilesHughes-MD-ZIP-Data-10-28-26.csv · every Maryland ZIP, every score, tier and treatment column, plus the demographics used in the companion report.
